How Particular works

Particles pull on each other at long range and push apart up close. Draw where they go and you get trails. This page explains that simulation, and then how atlas.py searches through 300,000 simulated worlds and keeps the best one of each kind on a map with 384 cells.

2 · ecosystems

Species and their rules

Particles can belong to species. A rule set gives two S×S matrices of multipliers: pull[a][b] and push[a][b] scale how a particle of species a reacts to one of species b. The matrices don't have to be symmetric, so species a can be drawn to b while b runs from a. A negative pull repels at long range.

The orbit preset has three species: a heavy core (gold), an inner ring (blue) and an outer ring (violet). The two rings follow the same rules, so the table groups them:

The rings feel the core's pull 30 times more strongly than the core feels theirs (6 against 0.2). The core stays near the middle, and the rings circle it because their sideways speed balances the pull. Without that speed they would fall straight in.

Hand-built scenes color trails by species. The atlas stills and the atlas clips below color by time, like the classic scenes, even when a world has several species.

3 · random_genome, build

A world as a genome

The search needs worlds it can draw at random and then change a little. A world is stored as a genome: a small JSON dict. build(genome) turns it into particle positions, velocities, species and force constants, and depends on nothing but the genome. The genome holds:

  • k, a symmetry order from 1 to 6.
  • pieces, 1 to 3 of them, each a ring, a set of satellites or a set of spokes with its own parameters. Satellites and spokes are repeated k times around the center.
  • log2_pull, log2_push, global force scales in log2 units from −2 to 2, so G and C can each be scaled from ¼× to 4×.
  • species, 1 to 3, and their log2_rules matrices, also from −2 to 2. Each piece names two species and alternates between them.
  • steps, the run length: 400 to 1400 in steps of 200. atlas.py adds this one.

This is the genome of the atlas world in cell (6, 4, 3), which section 8 mutates. It has a ring of species 2 and two satellites of species 1:

build() also cleans up the result. Rings are capped so neighbors start at least 0.8 units apart (this satellite asks for 31 particles but gets 15). Particles that start within 0.3 of an earlier one are dropped. A world with more than 260 particles is thinned to 260 using the random state stored in thin, so the same genome always keeps the same particles. Finally the center of mass is moved to the origin and the mean velocity is subtracted, so nothing drifts out of frame.

4 · measure()

Measuring a world: three axes

After a world runs, measure() reads its trajectory and returns three numbers that describe its behavior. These are the map's axes. It first picks a frame: the view half-width is 1.1 times the radius that contains 95% of the faded ink, the same rule the stills use. Measuring takes about 3 ms on one CPU core.

Orderly → chaotic

For every particle and frame, take the change in heading from one logged step to the next. A turn of , where a particle reverses along a straight line, is folded back to 0, so a particle bouncing in and out along a ray counts as moving straight. The axis value is the mean turn, weighted by the fade :

Calm orbits barely turn. Tangles turn sharply and often. Values span several decades, so this axis is binned on a log scale.

Sparse → dense

Rasterize the faded ink onto a 48×48 grid and count the share of cells holding more than a quarter of the average ink per cell. The threshold keeps faint stray loops from counting. This is search.py's fill score.

Circular → branching

This axis asks how much of the picture is lines pointing at the center. measure() draws the trails into a 128-pixel image, blurs it, and takes the brightness gradient at every pixel. A gradient that points along the radius belongs to an edge that runs around the center (a ring). A gradient at right angles to the radius belongs to an edge that runs out from the center (a ray).

The image is cut into polar cells: 16 rings by 32 sectors. Each cell scores its ray energy minus its ring energy. A cell only counts if that score stays positive over 5 consecutive rings in the same sector, so short ticks on a ring don't count and only long rays do. The axis value is the counted energy as a share of all edge energy. It is read at 128 pixels with 32 sectors and again at 32 pixels with 16 sectors, and the larger value is kept.

5 · calibrate, cell_of

Classifying: from three numbers to a cell

The map is a 3D grid: 8 bins of chaos × 8 bins of density × 6 bins of branching, 384 cells. A world's cell is found by looking up each feature against that axis's bin edges (np.searchsorted).

The edges are fixed once, from replicate 0's random genomes. For each axis, calibrate() takes the 1st and 99th percentiles of those genomes' values and spaces the interior edges evenly between them. Chaos is spaced evenly in log space. The bins at each end are open, so an extreme value still lands in bin 0 or the last bin.

The amber marks are the world from section 3. Its branching value is 0.3183 and the edge between bins 3 and 4 is at 0.3186, so it lands in bin 3 by 0.0003. Cells are sharp boundaries on continuous values, and a small change to a world can move it to a neighboring cell. Section 8 shows this happening.

7 · run_map_elites

MAP-Elites: one champion per cell

search.py, the earlier approach, ranks random setups by a single weighted score. The research notes found that 11 of its top 16 were a single ring: one score converges on one kind of answer. MAP-Elites (Mouret & Clune, 2015) keeps the best world in every cell of the behavior map instead, so the result is a variety of good worlds. The paper's main design lesson is to turn a preference into an axis rather than a fitness weight. That is why density and smoothness, which were weighted terms in search.py, are axes here.

The archive is a dict from cell to its elite. The loop:

  1. Seed. Draw 2,000 random genomes, run and measure them, and put each in its cell. When two land in the same cell, the fitter one stays.
  2. Select. Pick a filled cell uniformly at random and copy its elite's genome. Every filled cell has the same chance, so a weak elite in a rare cell gets as much attention as a strong one in a crowded region.
  3. Vary. With probability 0.15, take one piece from the elite of another cell at most 2 cells away on every axis (crossover). Otherwise apply one mutation operator, chosen by weight. Section 8 lists them.
  4. Evaluate and insert. Run the child and measure it. It takes cell b(child) if that cell is empty or it beats the elite there. Then go back to step 2. A run does 48,000 children after the 2,000 seeds.

The child often lands in a different cell from its parent. This is how the map fills: a cell's best world is often the child of an elite from a neighboring cell. The explorer in section 10 shows each elite's parent cell.

On a GPU, gpu_sim.simulate_batch runs a batch of 32 children at once in a single CUDA kernel, with four batches in flight, while a CPU pool builds setups and scores trajectories. Each batch is made from the archive as it is at that moment. On CPU, a process pool keeps 3 evaluations per worker queued and inserts results as they finish.

The random-sampling control

Each replicate first evaluates 50,000 purely random genomes, binned the same way. That is the control. MAP-Elites then runs for the same 50,000 evaluations, seeded with the first 2,000 of those random genomes. The published atlas ran 3 replicates of both: 300,000 simulations in 9 minutes on an RTX 5070 Ti. It keeps the best elite per cell across the three MAP-Elites runs.

8 · mutate

Mutation: how a child differs from its parent

Each operator changes one part of the genome. Gaussian steps are 6% of a gene's range for piece genes and 0.25 log2 units for forces and rules, clamped to range (angles wrap around). If a child ends up with fewer than 40 particles, mutate tries again, up to 20 times, and otherwise returns an unchanged copy, recorded as none.

9 · metrics

Results: MAP-Elites against random sampling

The paper's four metrics compare each run to the best fitness any run found in each cell, across all 6 runs (3 random, 3 MAP-Elites). 380 of the 384 cells were filled by at least one run.

  • Coverage: cells this run filled, out of the 380.
  • Reliability: the mean over all 380 cells of this run's fitness divided by the best known, with empty cells counting 0. The main measure.
  • Precision: the same mean, over only the cells this run filled.
  • Global: this run's best world divided by the best world overall.
Mean of 3 replicates, 50,000 evaluations each
random samplingMAP-Elites

Random sampling already finds 92% of the cells; MAP-Elites finds 98%. The bigger difference is quality within cells: reliability rises from 0.68 to 0.92. This matches what the research notes predicted from the paper: at a coarse grid, MAP-Elites helps more with quality per cell than with coverage.

The worlds are chaotic. CPU and GPU round differently, so the same genome can end up with a slightly different picture on each. Of the 379 elites, 371 land in the same cell when re-run on CPU for this page. The measurements quoted in the clip captions are from the CPU re-runs.
